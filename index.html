Australia's economy: job, spending and trade

By Chenul Punchihewa, October 2026

How are Australians doing? We look at who is in work, what households spend, which industries are growing and who buys what we sell.

In 2020 the economy, jobs and savings all shifted at once, then prices took off

In the June 2020 quarter the economy was smaller than a year earlier, unemployment reached 7.4% and households saved 23.3% of their income. Prices were barely moving: annual inflation was -0.3%.

Inflation then climbed to 7.9% by the end of 2022, while saving fell to 1.8% by September 2023. By June 2026, unemployment was 4.4%, inflation was 3.9%, saving was 6.5% and the economy had grown 2.1% over the year.

Each panel has its own vertical scale, so compare the shape of each line over time, not the height of one panel against another. Saving is the share of household income that is not spent. Unemployment is read at the end of each quarter.

Source: ABS, Australian National Accounts (June 2026), Labour Force, Australia (August 2026) and Consumer Price Index, Australia (August 2026). Infaltion is calculated from ABS CPI Index numbers and may differ from the published figure by about 0.1 percentage points.

The share of Australians in work fell sharply in 2020, then climbed higher than before

The employment ration is the share of the population aged 15 and over who are in work. In February 2020, it was 62.6%. By May 2020 it had fallen to 57.9%. It was back at its pre-COVID level by May 2021, then reached 64.4% at several points between 2023 and 2025. In August 2026 it was 63.9%.

Darker blue means a higher share in work. Figures are seasonally adjusted, and blank squares are months outside the data. Hover over a sqaure for the exact figure.

Source: ABS, Labour Force, Australia, August 2026 (seasonally adjusted).

Underemployment is higher than unemployment in every state and territory

In August 2026, 4.6% of Australian workers were unemployed and 6.3% were underemployed, meaning they had a job but wanted more hours. Tasmania was highest on both measures, at 5.2% and 7.1%.

The ACT had the lowest unemployment (4.0%) but the widest gap between the two measures. The Northern Territory was the only place where they were almost equal (4.8% and 4.9%).

Source: ABS, Labour Force, Australia, August 2026 (trend estimates).

We spend almost twice as many dollars each month as in 2012

Australian households spent about $82 billion in August 2026, up from about $44 billion in mid-2012. Spending fell by more than a fifth between February and April 2020 as COVID restrictions began, and had mostly recovered by June.

Figures are seasonally adjusted, which removes regular calendar effects such as Christmas shopping. They are in current prices and are not adjusted for inflation, so part of the rise reflects higher prices rather than people buying more.

Source: ABS, Monthly Household Spending Indicator, August 2026.

Most industries grew in the June quarter, but manufacturing fell the most

Of the 19 industries shown, 14 grew and 5 shrank. Professional, scientific and technical services grew the most (+2.3%). Manufacturing fell the most (-1.9%), followed by administrative and support services (-1.4%)

Gross value added (GVA) is the value an industry adds to the economy. Bars show the percentage change on the March quarter, adjusted for price changes and seasonal patterns. This is one quarter only, so it shows a snapshot rather than a trend.

Source: ABS, Australian National Accounts: National Income, Expenditure and Product, June 2026

One in every three dollars of goods we sell abroad goes to China

In 2025, Australia sold A$176 billion of goods to China. That is 33.7% of all goods exports and almost three times as much as to Japan (A$62 billion). The top four destinations together took about 61% of the total.

These figures cover goods only, not services such as tourism and education. Values are in current prices.

Source: DFAT, Merchandise trade statistical pivot tables (calendar year 2025)

From one in eight to one in three: China's growing share of our exports

China bought about 12% of our goods exports in 2006. That rose to about 41% in 2020 and easted to about 34% in 2025. Over the same period, total goods exports grew from A$164 billion to A$524 billion.

Each coloured band is a destination, and its thickness shows the value of exports. Read the chart for the overall pattern rather than exact figures. Values are in current prices. DFAT notes that some country totals are understated for confidentiality reasons.

Source: DFAT, Merchandise trade statistical pivot tables (calendar years 2006-2025).

Eating out has grown fastest since 2019, and alcohol and tobacco is the only category that shrank

Between August 2019 and August 2026, motnhly household spending across these nine categories rose from A$55 billion to A$82 billion, up 49%. Hotels, cafes and restaurants grew the most, by more than two-thirds, from A$6.8 billion to A$11.4 billion. Recreation and culture rose 62% and transport 56%.

Alcohol and tobacco was the only category to fall, from A$3.6 billion to A$2.9 billion, down about 20%.

Figures are seasonally adjusted and in current prices, so part of every rise is higher prices rather than people buying more. They cover nine ABS spending categories, not all household spending.

Source: ABS, Monthly Household Spending Indicator, August 2026 (seasonally adjusted).
