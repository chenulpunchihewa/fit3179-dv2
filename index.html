The Big Picture

In 2020 the economy, jobs and savings all shifted at once, then prices took off

How to read: Each panel has its own vertical scale, so compare the shapes of the lines, not their heights. The red dotted line marks the June 2020 quarter and the labels give each value then. Saving is the share of household income that is not spent.

In the June 2020 quarter the economy was smaller than a year earlier, unemployment reached 7.4% and households saved 23.3% of their income. Prices were barely moving: annual inflation was -0.3%.

Inflation then climbed to 7.9% by the end of 2022, while saving fell to 1.8% by September 2023. By June 2026, unemployment was 4.4%, inflation was 3.9%, saving was 6.5% and the economy had grown 2.1% over the year.

Source: ABS, Australian National Accounts (June 2026), Labour Force, Australia (August 2026) and Consumer Price Index, Australia (August 2026). Inflation is calculated from ABS CPI index numbers and may differ from the published figure by about 0.1 percentage points. Unemployment is the rate at the end of each quarter.

Work

Of every 100 Australians aged 15 and over, 64 have a job

How to read: Each person icon stands for one in every 100 people aged 15 and over, so the three colours add up to 100. Blue means in a job, red means unemployed and looking for work, and grey means not in the labour force.

In August 2026, about 64 of every 100 Australians aged 15 and over were in a job. Economists call this the employment ratio. Another 3 were out of work and looking for one. The other 33 were neither working nor looking for work, for example because they are retired or studying full time.

The unemployment rate of 4.6% is measured against the 67 in every 100 who are in the labour force, meaning they are working or looking for work, not against everyone. That is why 3 icons are red rather than 5.

Source: ABS, Labour Force, Australia, August 2026 (trend estimates).

The share of Australians in work fell sharply in 2020, then climbed higher than before

How to read: Each block is one month and each row is one year. Darker blue means a higher share of people in work, and grey blocks are months outside the data. Figures are seasonally adjusted. Hover over a block for the exact figure.

The employment ratio is the share of people aged 15 and over who are in a job, like the 64 in every 100 above. In February 2020 it was 62.2%. By May 2020 it had fallen to 57.9%. It was back at its pre-COVID level by May 2021, dipped again in late 2021, then reached 64.4% at several points between 2023 and 2025.

Source: ABS, Labour Force, Australia, August 2026 (seasonally adjusted).

More than half of Australia's workers live in New South Wales and Victoria

How to read: Each circle is a state or territory. Its area shows how many people are employed, not how much land there is, and its colour shows the employment ratio. Circles sit near their real location but are nudged apart so none overlap, so distances are not exact.

In August 2026, New South Wales (4.6 million) and Victoria (3.8 million) had about 57% of Australia's employed people between them. Adding Queensland takes the share to 77%.

The Northern Territory (69.3%) and the ACT (68.8%) have the highest employment ratios, even though theirs are the smallest circles. Tasmania is lowest at 57.5%. The three biggest states are all close to the national figure of 63.9%.

Source: ABS, Labour Force, Australia, August 2026 (trend estimates).

Underemployment is higher than unemployment in every state and territory

How to read: Each row is a state or territory, plus Australia as a whole. The red dot is the unemployment rate and the amber dot is the underemployment rate. Both are shares of the labour force, which means everyone who is working or looking for work.

In August 2026, 4.6% of Australian workers were unemployed and 6.3% were underemployed, meaning they had a job but wanted more hours. Tasmania was highest on both measures, at 5.2% and 7.1%.

The ACT had the lowest unemployment (4.0%) but the widest gap between the two measures. The Northern Territory was the only place where they were almost equal (4.8% and 4.9%).

Adding the two measures together shows how much of each workforce is not fully used. The next map does exactly that.

Source: ABS, Labour Force, Australia, August 2026 (trend estimates).

In Tasmania and Victoria, about one in eight workers is out of work or wants more hours

How to read: Each state is coloured by its underutilisation rate. Darker red means more unused labour. Both rates are measured against the labour force, which means everyone who is working or looking for work. Hover over a state to see both parts.

In August 2026, 10.9% of Australia's labour force was either out of work (4.6%) or wanted more hours (6.3%). Tasmania was highest at 12.3%, followed by Victoria at 12.0%. The Northern Territory (9.7%) and New South Wales (9.8%) were lowest.

The ABS calls this the underutilisation rate: the unemployment rate and the underemployment rate added together. Adding the rounded rates can differ from the ABS's own figure by 0.1 percentage points.

Source: ABS, Labour Force, Australia, August 2026 (trend estimates). Boundaries: Natural Earth.

Prices and Spending

Prices rose fastest in Hobart and Adelaide, and slowest in Melbourne

How to read: The size of each circle shows the rate of price rises over the past year. The colour shows whether the city is above (red) or below (blue) the national rate of 4.0%.

In the year to August 2026, prices rose 4.8% in Hobart and 4.7% in Adelaide. Melbourne had the smallest rise, at 3.5%, followed by Sydney at 3.9%. Those two were the only capital cities below the national rate of 4.0%.

The cities only range from 3.5% to 4.8%, so the circles look similar in size. The labels give the exact figures.

Source: ABS, Consumer Price Index, Australia, August 2026. City positions are approximate city centres. Boundaries: Natural Earth.

Eating out collapsed in 2020, then grew faster than any other kind of spending

How to read: Each line is one kind of household spending compared with its level in August 2019, which is set at 100. A line above 100 means people are spending more than they did then. The grey lines are the other seven categories. Hover over a line to see its category.

The black line, all spending together, dropped to 76 in April 2020, was back above its February 2020 level by July 2020, and by August 2026 was 49% higher than in August 2019. The dashed line, consumer prices overall (CPI), rose about 28% between September 2019 and June 2026.

Spending on hotels, cafes and restaurants (blue) fell the furthest, to 43 in April 2020, and by August 2026 was 68% higher than in 2019, the biggest rise of the nine categories. Alcohol and tobacco (red) is the exception: it slipped back to its 2019 level in early 2024 and by August 2026 was about 20% lower.

Note: Figures are seasonally adjusted and in current prices, so part of every rise is higher prices. The nine categories are the ones the ABS publishes and leave out things like rent, electricity and education, so they are not all household spending. CPI measures a different basket of goods, so compare the lines roughly.

Source: ABS, Monthly Household Spending Indicator, August 2026 (seasonally adjusted); ABS, Consumer Price Index, Australia (quarterly index).

Transport and recreation added the most to monthly spending, about A$5 billion each

How to read: Each line joins one kind of spending in August 2019 (left) to August 2026 (right), in A$ billion a month. The number beside each name is the change. The blue lines are the two biggest rises and the red line is the only fall.

Between August 2019 and August 2026, monthly household spending across the nine categories rose from A$55 billion to A$82 billion, an increase of A$27 billion. Transport and recreation and culture each added about A$5.3 billion a month, together nearly two-fifths of the rise. Eating out added A$4.6 billion and food A$4.2 billion.

Alcohol and tobacco was the only category to fall, by A$0.7 billion.

Note: Figures are seasonally adjusted and in current prices, so part of every rise is higher prices rather than people buying more. They cover nine ABS spending categories, not all household spending.

Source: ABS, Monthly Household Spending Indicator, August 2026 (seasonally adjusted).

Industries and Trade

Most industries grew in the June quarter, but manufacturing fell the most

How to read: Each bar is one industry. Blue bars to the right grew over the quarter and red bars to the left shrank. The bars show the percentage change on the March quarter, adjusted for price changes and seasonal patterns.

Of the 19 industries shown, 14 grew and 5 shrank. Professional, scientific and technical services grew the most (+2.3%). Manufacturing fell the most (-1.9%), followed by administrative and support services (-1.4%).

Gross value added (GVA) is the value an industry adds to the economy. This is one quarter only, so it shows a snapshot rather than a trend.

Source: ABS, Australian National Accounts: National Income, Expenditure and Product, June 2026.

China buys more of our exports than Japan, South Korea and the United States combined

How to read: On the map, line width shows the value of exports, and each line runs from the middle of Australia to the middle of the destination country, so the lines are not shipping routes. The bars give the exact figure for each of the 15 largest destinations. Hover over a line, dot or bar for the exact figures.

In 2025, Australia sold A$176 billion of goods to China. That is 33.7% of all goods exports, or one dollar in every three, and almost three times as much as to Japan (A$62 billion). China's total is more than Japan, South Korea (A$42 billion) and the United States (A$41 billion) combined, and these four destinations took about 61% of everything we sold overseas.

The 15 largest destinations together make up about 89% of exports. These figures cover goods only, not services such as tourism and education, and are in current prices.

Source: DFAT, Merchandise trade statistical pivot tables (calendar year 2025). Boundaries: Natural Earth.

From one in eight to one in three: China's growing share of our exports

How to read: Each coloured band is a destination, and its thickness shows the value of exports to it. Read the chart for the overall pattern rather than exact figures. Values are in current prices. Hover over a band to see its destination.

China bought about 12% of our goods exports in 2006. That rose to about 41% in 2020 and eased to about 34% in 2025.

Over the same period, total goods exports grew from A$164 billion to A$524 billion, and exports to China grew from about A$20 billion to A$176 billion.

Source: DFAT, Merchandise trade statistical pivot tables (calendar years 2006-2025).
